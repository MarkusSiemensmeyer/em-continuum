Fewer surprises
in delivery.

A shared picture for business and IT — and a small, reversible way to find out whether it helps us.

Set the frame immediately: this is not a pitch for a methodology, it is a proposal to test whether a specific practice reduces problems we already have. Everything in this deck is meant to be questioned.

Let’s start with the problems,
not with a method.

  • 01Requirements become clear only after development has started.
  • 02Business and IT read the same requirement — and mean different things.
  • 03Modernization efforts grow unexpectedly once we look inside.
  • 04Estimates get buffers because nobody fully trusts them.
  • 05Projects deliver — but not quite what was expected.
Ask the room: which of these have we seen in the last twelve months? Let them pick. If none apply, the rest of the deck is unnecessary — say that openly. These are cost, schedule and capacity issues, not methodology issues.

Cost of fixing a misunderstandingfound late
Nobody in this chain is doing a bad job. Each hand-off is reasonable on its own; the loss accumulates. The later a misunderstanding surfaces, the more it costs. The goal: make misunderstandings visible on the left side of this picture.

In one sentence

Business and IT review the same workflow — before we build it.

Event Modeling is simply a disciplined way of drawing that workflow so that everyone can read it and nothing important is left implicit.

This is the slide to remember. If someone only takes away one thing, it should be this sentence — not the name of the method.

Deliberately a non-IT example. No notation lecture needed: screens, actions, facts, information shown back. Business people can read this after thirty seconds. The interesting conversation is about the gaps: rejection, cancellation, overlapping requests.

No separate documents to keep in sync. Fewer translations, fewer places for meaning to get lost.

Today each role tends to own its own artefact: requirements doc, tickets, test plan, architecture diagram. They drift. Here they point at the same thing.

Outcomes you can evaluate.

Clearer requirements

Gaps and contradictions surface in a workshop, not in a sprint review.

Less rework

Misunderstandings are fixed on a whiteboard, where they are cheap.

Honest progress

Reported as working business behaviour, not as percentages.

Fewer surprises

Scope is visible up front — including the parts nobody mentioned.

None of these require leadership to understand the notation. They are things we can observe and, to a reasonable degree, measure — see the pilot slide.

With slices

9 of 14 workflow steps work end to end.

The remaining 5 are named, sized and visible on the same picture.

A slice is one column of the storyline: one thing a user can do and see. Done means demonstrable. This can run alongside existing status reporting — it does not have to replace it.

Address misunderstandings before they travel up the management chain. If Event Sourcing comes up: it pairs well with Event Modeling, but it is an implementation option, not a prerequisite, and not part of this proposal.

It isn’t free.
Here is what it costs.

Time up front

Workshops before building. Feels slower in week one; the bet is that it is faster by week six.

Business availability

Without a domain expert in the room, the model is just our guess — drawn nicely.

Keeping it current

A model nobody updates becomes another outdated document. It needs an owner.

If the pilot shows these costs outweigh the benefits for us, we stop. That is a valid result.

Being explicit about costs is what makes the rest credible. Also mention the learning curve: small for readers, a few weeks for the people facilitating.

A small, bounded pilot.

Weeks 1–2

Model together

Two or three workshops with business, development and test on one real feature.

Weeks 3–7

Build slice by slice

Deliver and demo working steps. Report progress as slices — next to the usual status, not instead of it.

Week 8

Review honestly

Compare against the agreed signals. Continue, adjust or stop.

One team · one feature with real business complexity · no change to budget or governance.

Pick a feature where misunderstanding is likely — not a trivial one (proves nothing) and not the most critical project of the year (too much pressure). Timeline is a suggestion; adjust to our sprint rhythm.

Three things.

1

A business expert

Two to three hours a week, mostly in the first two weeks.

2

Room to report differently

Progress in slices, alongside the existing reporting.

3

A review date

Eight weeks from start. We decide together, based on the signals.

Keep the ask small and concrete. No headcount, no tooling budget, no reorganization.

Less, but better.
